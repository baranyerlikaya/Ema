e^(λt) for real and complex λ

How e^{λt} behaves when λ is real (growth or decay), imaginary (rotation, oscillation) or complex (a growing or shrinking spiral), and why it is the key to linear equations of motion.

In short

[Basic explanation]

Theory

The defining property

For any constant $\lambda$, the chain rule gives [1, §The Exponential Function]:

$$\frac{d}{dt}e^{\lambda t} = \lambda\,e^{\lambda t}.$$

Read the other way round: $x(t) = e^{\lambda t}$ is the solution of $\dot x = \lambda x$ with $x(0) = 1$. Its rate of change is always proportional to its current value, with factor $\lambda$. MIT's notes call this the exponential principle and use it as the definition of $e^{\lambda t}$, so that it holds for complex $\lambda$ too [2, §6.1, §6.2].

An exponential is never zero. That is what lets you cancel it when you substitute $e^{\lambda t}$ into a linear equation of motion [2, §6.1].

Real λ: growth and decay

If $\lambda$ is real, $e^{\lambda t}$ is a positive real function. For $\lambda > 0$ it grows exponentially, and for $\lambda < 0$ it decays towards zero. A particle with position $x(t) = e^{\lambda t}$ always has velocity $\lambda x$: proportional to its position [1].

Imaginary λ = iω: rotation

For $\lambda = i\omega$ with $\omega$ real, Euler's formula gives [1, §Complex Exponential Function]; [2, §6.2]

$$e^{i\omega t} = \cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t.$$

This point has modulus $1$ for all $t$, so it moves around the unit circle in the complex plane with angular velocity $\omega$ (counterclockwise for $\omega > 0$) [2, §6.3]. Its real part $\cos\omega t$ and imaginary part $\sin\omega t$ are oscillations along two perpendicular axes; together they make the circular motion [1].

The derivative is $\frac{d}{dt}e^{i\omega t} = i\omega\,e^{i\omega t}$. The factor $i$ turns the velocity $90^\circ$ from the position, exactly as in uniform circular motion, and $|\dot z| = \omega|z|$ [1].

Complex λ = α + iω: spirals

For a general complex $\lambda = \alpha + i\omega$, the exponential law splits the exponential into a real factor and a rotating factor [2, §6.2]:

$$e^{(\alpha+i\omega)t} = e^{\alpha t}\left(\cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t\right).$$

The modulus is $e^{\alpha t}$ and the argument is $\omega t$ [2, §6.3]. So the real part $\alpha$ sets the size and the imaginary part $\omega$ sets the rotation:

  • $\alpha > 0$: a spiral running outwards, growing exponentially while it winds;
  • $\alpha < 0$: a spiral decaying towards the origin;
  • $\alpha = 0$: a circle; and if $\omega = 0$, the point stays on the positive real axis.

This is the combination of exponential growth or decay with oscillation, and it is what damped oscillators look like [1]; [2, §6.3].

Getting real motion back

Physical positions are real. The bridge is the reality principle: if a complex function $z = x + iy$ solves a linear equation whose coefficients are real, then its real part $x$ and its imaginary part $y$ are each real solutions [2, §6.2]. This works because differentiation acts on the real and imaginary parts separately.

So from $e^{(\alpha+i\omega)t}$ you get the two real functions $e^{\alpha t}\cos\omega t$ and $e^{\alpha t}\sin\omega t$. In practice: do the calculation with one complex exponential, and take the real part at the end [1, Worked Example "Oscillatory Motion and the Complex Exponential"]; [2, §6.2].

Note

Only the real part of $\lambda$ changes the size of $e^{\lambda t}$: $|e^{(\alpha+i\omega)t}| = e^{\alpha t}$. The imaginary part only rotates it [2, §6.3].

Warning

Take the real part only at the very end, after all derivatives and linear operations. Products are different: $\operatorname{Re}(z_1 z_2) \ne \operatorname{Re}(z_1)\operatorname{Re}(z_2)$ in general, so quantities that are products of two oscillations (such as energy or power) must be computed from the real functions [2, §5.1].

Worked examples

Example

Let $z(t) = A\,e^{i(\omega t+\phi)}$ with real constants $A$, $\omega$, $\phi$. Find its real and imaginary parts and the curve it traces, compute $\dot z$ and $\ddot z$, and show that $\ddot z + \omega^2 z = 0$. (From [1], Worked Example "Oscillatory Motion and the Complex Exponential".)

  1. Euler's formula: $z = A\cos(\omega t+\phi) + iA\sin(\omega t+\phi)$. So $x = A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ and $y = A\sin(\omega t+\phi)$.

  2. $x^2 + y^2 = A^2(\cos^2 + \sin^2) = A^2$: the point moves on a circle of radius $A$ about the origin.

  3. Differentiate (exponential principle with $\lambda = i\omega$): $\dot z = i\omega z$ and $\ddot z = (i\omega)^2 z = -\omega^2 z$.

  4. Hence $\ddot z + \omega^2 z = 0$. Splitting into real and imaginary parts gives $\ddot x + \omega^2 x = 0$ and $\ddot y + \omega^2 y = 0$: both components separately perform simple harmonic motion.

Check

Directly: $x = A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ gives $\ddot x = -A\omega^2\cos(\omega t+\phi) = -\omega^2 x$. ✓

Example

Describe $z(t) = e^{(-1+i)t}$ and give two real functions obtained from it. (From [2], §6.2.)

  1. Here $\alpha = -1$ and $\omega = 1$: $z(t) = e^{-t}(\cos t + i\sin t)$.

  2. Its modulus $e^{-t}$ shrinks while its argument $t$ grows, so $z$ spirals counterclockwise into the origin.

  3. Real part $e^{-t}\cos t$ and imaginary part $e^{-t}\sin t$: oscillations whose amplitude decays exponentially. In [2] both are shown to solve $\ddot x + 2\dot x + 2x = 0$, whose characteristic roots are $-1 \pm i$.

Check

At $t = \pi$: $z = e^{-\pi}(\cos\pi + i\sin\pi) = -e^{-\pi} \approx -0.043$. Half a turn later the point is on the negative real axis and much closer to the origin. ✓

Example

A point moves in the complex plane as $z(t) = e^{(\alpha+i\omega)t}$. Show that its speed is $\sqrt{\alpha^2+\omega^2}\,|z|$. (From [1], §Complex Exponential Function.)

  1. By the exponential principle, $\dot z = (\alpha + i\omega)\,z$.

  2. Moduli multiply, so $|\dot z| = |\alpha + i\omega|\,|z| = \sqrt{\alpha^2+\omega^2}\,|z|$.

Check

For $\alpha = 0$ this gives $|\dot z| = \omega|z|$, the speed $v = R\omega$ of uniform circular motion with radius $R = |z|$. For $\omega = 0$ it gives $|\dot z| = |\alpha|\,|z|$, pure growth or decay. ✓
