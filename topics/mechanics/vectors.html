Vectors: Dot & Cross Product

Quantities with magnitude and direction: how to add them, split them into components, and multiply them with the dot product (a number) and the cross product (a vector).

In short

Some things just have a size, like your mass or the temperature. These are scalars. Other things have a size and a direction, like "walk 5 metres north" or "push to the left". These are vectors, drawn as arrows [1]; [2].

Adding arrows: put the second arrow's tail on the first arrow's tip. The sum goes from the very start to the very end [2]. In numbers, add the matching parts: $x$ with $x$, $y$ with $y$, $z$ with $z$ [1]. Careful: lengths don't simply add. Walking 4 km east and then 3 km north puts you only 5 km from home, not 7 [1].

Two ways to multiply arrows:

  • Dot product, $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B = AB\cos\theta = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z$. The answer is a number. It asks "how much do these arrows point the same way?" It is biggest when they are lined up, zero when they are at right angles, and negative when they point against each other. Work is a dot product: $W = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d$ [1]; [3].
  • Cross product, $\mathbf A\times\mathbf B$. The answer is a new arrow, standing straight up from both. Its length $AB\sin\theta$ is the area of the parallelogram the two arrows make. Find its direction with your right hand: curl your fingers from $\mathbf A$ to $\mathbf B$, and your thumb points the way [1]; [2].

Order matters for the cross product: $\mathbf B\times\mathbf A = -\mathbf A\times\mathbf B$ (the thumb flips). For lined-up arrows it is zero [2]. Turning a wrench uses it: the torque is $\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf r\times\mathbf F$ [1].

Theory

Scalars and vectors

A scalar is described completely by one number with a unit, such as mass, temperature or energy. A vector has both a magnitude and a direction, and is drawn as an arrow whose length is the magnitude [1, §Scalars, §Vectors]; [2, §3.1]. Position, displacement, velocity, acceleration, force and momentum are vectors [2, §3.1.1].

We write vectors in bold, $\mathbf A$, or with an arrow, $\vec A$. The magnitude is $|\mathbf A|$ or simply $A$ [1].

Addition and scalar multiplication

To add $\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf B$ geometrically, place the tail of $\mathbf B$ at the tip of $\mathbf A$; the sum runs from the tail of $\mathbf A$ to the tip of $\mathbf B$. Equivalently, draw both from the same point: the sum is the diagonal of the parallelogram they span [2, §3.1.2]. Addition is commutative and associative, $\mathbf A + \mathbf B = \mathbf B + \mathbf A$ [2, §3.1.2].

Multiplying by a scalar $c$ stretches the vector by $|c|$; a negative $c$ also reverses its direction [1, §Scalar Multiplication].

In components, both operations act term by term [1, §Components of Vectors, §Unit Vectors]:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf A + \mathbf B &= (A_x + B_x)\hat\imath + (A_y + B_y)\hat\jmath \\ &\quad + (A_z + B_z)\hat k, \\ c\mathbf A &= cA_x\hat\imath + cA_y\hat\jmath + cA_z\hat k.\end{aligned}$$

A unit vector in the direction of $\mathbf A$ is $\hat{\mathbf a} = \mathbf A/|\mathbf A|$ [1, §Unit Vectors].

The dot (scalar) product

The dot product of two vectors is a number. With $\theta$ the angle between them ($0 \le \theta \le \pi$) [1, §Dot Product]; [3, §13.8.1]:

$$\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B = AB\cos\theta = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z.$$

The component formula follows from $\hat\imath\cdot\hat\imath = \hat\jmath\cdot\hat\jmath = \hat k\cdot\hat k = 1$ and $\hat\imath\cdot\hat\jmath = \hat\jmath\cdot\hat k = \hat\imath\cdot\hat k = 0$ [3, §13.8.1].

What it is good for [1]; [3, §13.8]:

  • angles: $\cos\theta = \dfrac{\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B}{AB}$; the product is positive for acute angles, negative for obtuse ones, and zero exactly when the vectors are perpendicular;
  • length: $A = \sqrt{\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf A}$;
  • projection: $A\cos\theta = \mathbf A\cdot\hat{\mathbf b}$ is the component of $\mathbf A$ along $\mathbf B$;
  • work: a constant force $\mathbf F$ acting along a displacement $\mathbf d$ does work $W = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d$.

The cross (vector) product

The cross product of two vectors is a vector perpendicular to both. Its magnitude is [1, §Cross Product]; [2, §3.4]

$$|\mathbf A\times\mathbf B| = AB\sin\theta,$$

which is the area of the parallelogram spanned by $\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf B$. Its direction is given by the right-hand rule: curl the fingers of your right hand from $\mathbf A$ towards $\mathbf B$; your thumb points along $\mathbf A\times\mathbf B$ [2, §3.4.1].

Properties [2, §3.4.2]: it is anticommutative, $\mathbf B\times\mathbf A = -\mathbf A\times\mathbf B$; it is distributive over addition; and it vanishes for parallel vectors. For the unit vectors, $\hat\imath\times\hat\jmath = \hat k$, $\hat\jmath\times\hat k = \hat\imath$, $\hat k\times\hat\imath = \hat\jmath$, and $\hat\imath\times\hat\imath = 0$ etc. Expanding term by term gives [1, §Cross Product]:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf A\times\mathbf B = {}&(A_yB_z - A_zB_y)\,\hat\imath \\ &+ (A_zB_x - A_xB_z)\,\hat\jmath \\ &+ (A_xB_y - A_yB_x)\,\hat k.\end{aligned}$$

This is easiest to remember as a determinant with rows $(\hat\imath, \hat\jmath, \hat k)$, $(A_x, A_y, A_z)$, $(B_x, B_y, B_z)$ [1, Worked Example 4]. In mechanics the cross product gives the torque of a force, $\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf r\times\mathbf F$ [1, Worked Example 4].

Note

Dot product → a scalar, largest when the vectors are parallel. Cross product → a vector, largest when they are perpendicular. Use the dot product for work and angles, and the cross product for torque and areas [1]; [2, §3.4]; [3, §13.8].

Warning

Magnitudes don't add: $|\mathbf A + \mathbf B| \ne |\mathbf A| + |\mathbf B|$ unless the vectors point the same way. And the order matters in a cross product: $\mathbf A\times\mathbf B = -\mathbf B\times\mathbf A$ [1, Worked Example 2]; [2, §3.4.2].

Visualization

Arrow $\mathbf A$ (dark) is fixed; arrow $\mathbf B$ (grey) turns around it. The thick segment under $\mathbf A$ is the projection of $\mathbf B$ onto $\mathbf A$; the dot product is $A$ times this (signed) length, $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B = AB\cos\theta$. It is largest when the arrows line up, zero at $90^\circ$, and negative beyond. The shaded parallelogram has area $|\mathbf A\times\mathbf B| = AB|\sin\theta|$. The cross product points out of the page while $\mathbf B$ is counterclockwise from $\mathbf A$ ($0 < \theta < 180^\circ$), and into the page otherwise (right-hand rule).

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

Let $\mathbf A = 3\hat\imath - \hat\jmath + 2\hat k$ and $\mathbf B = -\hat\imath + 4\hat\jmath + 2\hat k$. Find $\mathbf A + \mathbf B$, $|\mathbf A + \mathbf B|$ and $2\mathbf A - 3\mathbf B$. Then find the force $\mathbf F_3$ that keeps a particle in equilibrium under $\mathbf F_1 = (5\hat\imath + 2\hat\jmath)\ \text{N}$ and $\mathbf F_2 = (-3\hat\imath + 6\hat\jmath)\ \text{N}$. (From [1], Worked Example 2.)

  1. Add components: $\mathbf A + \mathbf B = 2\hat\imath + 3\hat\jmath + 4\hat k$, so $|\mathbf A + \mathbf B| = \sqrt{4 + 9 + 16} = \sqrt{29} \approx 5.39$.

  2. $2\mathbf A = 6\hat\imath - 2\hat\jmath + 4\hat k$ and $3\mathbf B = -3\hat\imath + 12\hat\jmath + 6\hat k$, so $2\mathbf A - 3\mathbf B = 9\hat\imath - 14\hat\jmath - 2\hat k$.

  3. Equilibrium means $\mathbf F_1 + \mathbf F_2 + \mathbf F_3 = 0$, so $\mathbf F_3 = -(2\hat\imath + 8\hat\jmath) = (-2\hat\imath - 8\hat\jmath)\ \text{N}$, with magnitude $\sqrt{68} = 2\sqrt{17} \approx 8.2\ \text{N}$.

Check

$|\mathbf A| + |\mathbf B| = \sqrt{14} + \sqrt{21} \approx 8.3$, much more than $|\mathbf A + \mathbf B| \approx 5.39$: magnitudes don't simply add.

Example

Let $\mathbf A = 2\hat\imath + 3\hat\jmath - \hat k$ and $\mathbf B = \hat\imath - 2\hat\jmath + 2\hat k$. Find $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B$, the angle between them, and the projection of $\mathbf A$ on $\mathbf B$. Also find the work done by $\mathbf F = (3\hat\imath + 4\hat\jmath)\ \text{N}$ over $\mathbf d = (2\hat\imath - \hat\jmath)\ \text{m}$. (From [1], Worked Example 3.)

  1. $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B = 2 - 6 - 2 = -6$. With $|\mathbf A| = \sqrt{14}$ and $|\mathbf B| = 3$:

    $$\cos\theta = \frac{-6}{3\sqrt{14}} \approx -0.535,$$

    so $\theta \approx 122^\circ$.

  2. Projection on $\mathbf B$: $A_\parallel = \mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B/|\mathbf B| = -6/3 = -2$. The minus sign means $\mathbf A$ points partly against $\mathbf B$.

  3. Work: $W = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d = (3)(2) + (4)(-1) = 2\ \text{J}$.

Check

A negative dot product goes with an obtuse angle, and $122^\circ > 90^\circ$. ✓

Example

Let $\mathbf A = 2\hat\imath + \hat\jmath - \hat k$ and $\mathbf B = \hat\imath + 2\hat\jmath + \hat k$. Compute $\mathbf A\times\mathbf B$, the area of the parallelogram they span, and the torque $\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf r\times\mathbf F$ for $\mathbf r = (2\hat\imath + \hat\jmath)\ \text{m}$ and $\mathbf F = 3\hat\jmath\ \text{N}$. (From [1], Worked Example 4.)

  1. Determinant:

    $$\begin{aligned}\mathbf A\times\mathbf B &= \begin{vmatrix}\hat\imath & \hat\jmath & \hat k\\ 2 & 1 & -1\\ 1 & 2 & 1\end{vmatrix} \\ &= \hat\imath(1+2) - \hat\jmath(2+1) \\ &\quad + \hat k(4-1) \\ &= 3\hat\imath - 3\hat\jmath + 3\hat k.\end{aligned}$$

  2. Area $= |\mathbf A\times\mathbf B| = \sqrt{27} = 3\sqrt3 \approx 5.20$.

  3. Torque: $\boldsymbol\tau = (2\hat\imath + \hat\jmath)\times 3\hat\jmath = 6\,(\hat\imath\times\hat\jmath) + 3\,(\hat\jmath\times\hat\jmath) = 6\hat k\ \text{N·m}$. The part of $\mathbf r$ parallel to $\mathbf F$ contributes nothing.

Check

$\mathbf A\cdot(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B) = 6 - 3 - 3 = 0$: the cross product is perpendicular to $\mathbf A$. ✓ Also $|\mathbf A| = |\mathbf B| = \sqrt6$ gives $\sin\theta = 3\sqrt3/6 = \sqrt3/2$, and $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B = 3$ gives $\cos\theta = 1/2$: both mean $\theta = 60^\circ$.
