Friction, Drag, Free Fall, Terminal Speed

Dry friction between surfaces, fluid drag that depends on speed, free fall with and without air resistance, and the terminal speed where gravity and drag balance.

In short

Friction between two solids comes in two flavours [1]; [2]:

  • Kinetic (things actually sliding): a fixed amount, $f_k = \mu_k N$, pushing against the motion.
  • Static (not sliding yet): whatever is needed to hold things still, up to a maximum $\mu_s N$. Static friction is a little stronger than kinetic — that's why things are hard to start moving but easier to keep moving.

Here $N$ is how hard the surfaces press together, and $\mu$ is a number between about 0 (ice) and 1 (rubber) that depends on the two materials.

Drag through air or water depends on how fast you go [1]; [2]:

  • Slow (sticky): drag is proportional to speed, $F_r = -kv$. Think of marbles in syrup, or sand grains in water.
  • Fast: drag is proportional to speed squared, $F_r \propto v^2$. Think of cars, cyclists, skydivers.

Dropping something.

  • No air: it falls with acceleration $g$, hits the ground at speed $\sqrt{2gh}$, regardless of weight [1].
  • With air drag: as it speeds up, drag grows. Eventually drag = gravity, and the speed stops growing. That's the terminal speed [1]:

    $$v_\infty = \frac{mg}{k}.$$

This is why a feather falls slowly and a hammer fast: in air, their terminal speeds are very different. On the Moon (no air) they fall together [1].

With linear drag, the body approaches $v_\infty$ smoothly: $v(t) = v_\infty(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, with $\tau = m/k$ the "characteristic time". After a few $\tau$, you're essentially at terminal speed [1].

Theory

Dry friction

Between two solid surfaces, friction has two regimes [1, Worked Example “Motion on an Inclined Plane and Friction Forces”]; [2, §8.3.2]:

  • Kinetic (surfaces sliding): $f_k = \mu_k N$, opposite to the relative motion;
  • Static (surfaces at rest): $0 \le f_s \le \mu_s N$, whatever it takes to prevent slipping.

Both are proportional to the normal force $N$ and are, to a good approximation, independent of the contact area and (for ordinary speeds) of the sliding speed. Usually $\mu_s \gtrsim \mu_k$ [2, §8.3.2].

Fluid drag

In a fluid (air or liquid), moving bodies feel a drag force opposing their velocity. Two useful models [1, Worked Example “A ‘More Realistic’ Free Fall and Terminal Speed”]; [2, §8.6]:

  • Linear (low speed, viscous): $\mathbf F_r = -k\mathbf v$. For a sphere of radius $R$ in a fluid of viscosity $\eta$, Stokes' law gives $k = 6\pi\eta R$ [2, §8.6, Example 8.5].
  • Quadratic (high speed, inertial): $\mathbf F_r = -\tfrac12C\rho A\,v\,\mathbf v$, with $C$ a drag coefficient, $\rho$ fluid density and $A$ the cross-section area [2, §8.6, Example 8.6].

Free fall without air

Dropping a body from height $h$ with no air resistance, Newton's second law gives $\ddot y = -g$. Starting from rest at $y_0 = h$ [1, Worked Example “Free fall Motion”]:

$$\begin{gathered}y(t) = h - \tfrac12gt^2, \qquad v_y(t) = -gt, \\ t_{\text{land}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{2h}{g}}, \qquad v = \sqrt{2gh}.\end{gathered}$$

Independent of the mass [1].

Free fall with linear drag

Now add linear drag: $m\dot v = -mg - kv$ (positive $v$ upward). Rearrange and recognize a first-order linear ODE [1, Worked Example “A ‘More Realistic’ Free Fall and Terminal Speed”]:

$$\frac{dv}{dt} + \frac{k}{m}v = -g.$$

Solve with the integrating factor $e^{kt/m}$ (or by separating variables around $v = -mg/k$). With $v(0) = 0$:

$$v(t) = -\frac{mg}{k}\left(1 - e^{-kt/m}\right).$$

Reading off: speed grows from zero toward $v_\infty = mg/k$ with time constant $\tau = m/k$ [1].

Terminal speed

The terminal speed is reached when drag balances gravity, $m\dot v = 0$: $-mg - kv_\infty = 0$ gives [1]

$$v_\infty = \frac{mg}{k}.$$

Below $v_\infty$ gravity wins and the body speeds up; above $v_\infty$ drag wins and it slows down; at $v_\infty$ the forces balance and the speed stays constant [1]; [2, Example 8.5].

For quadratic drag the balance $mg = \tfrac12C\rho Av_\infty^2$ gives $v_\infty = \sqrt{\tfrac{2mg}{C\rho A}}$ instead [2, §8.6]. The heavier the body (for the same shape), the higher the terminal speed.

Note

With linear drag, after $t \approx 3\tau$ the body is within about 5% of $v_\infty$. A small sand grain in oil reaches it almost immediately; a raindrop a few hundred metres below a cloud [2, §8.6].

Warning

Drag depends on the relative velocity between the body and the fluid. If the air moves with the body (wind), use the velocity difference. In linear drag, $\mathbf F_r = -k(\mathbf v - \mathbf v_{\text{fluid}})$ [2, §8.6].

Visualization

A body dropped from rest with linear air drag $F_r = -kv$. The curve shows $v(t) = v_\infty(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ with $v_\infty = mg/k$ (the dashed line) and $\tau = m/k$ (the characteristic time). The dark dot marks the current moment. Increase $k$ (more drag): $v_\infty$ drops and the body reaches it faster. Increase $m$: $v_\infty$ rises, approach gets slower.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

A particle of mass $m$ is released from rest and falls under gravity with linear air drag $F_r = -kv$. Find $v(t)$ and the terminal speed. (From [1], Worked Example “A ‘More Realistic’ Free Fall and Terminal Speed”.)

  1. Second law (taking $v$ positive upward, so a falling body has $v < 0$): $m\dot v = -mg - kv$, i.e. $\dot v + (k/m)v = -g$.

  2. Integrating factor $e^{kt/m}$: $\dfrac{d}{dt}(ve^{kt/m}) = -ge^{kt/m}$, so $ve^{kt/m} = -\dfrac{mg}{k}e^{kt/m} + C$.

  3. $v(0) = 0$ gives $C = mg/k$, so $v(t) = -\dfrac{mg}{k}\left(1 - e^{-kt/m}\right)$.

  4. Terminal (set $\dot v = 0$): $v_\infty = -mg/k$, downward, with magnitude $mg/k$.

Check

$t = 0$: $v = 0$ ✓. $t \to \infty$: $v \to -mg/k$ ✓. $k \to 0$: expand $e^{-kt/m} \approx 1 - kt/m$ to get $v \to -gt$, the free-fall limit ✓.

Example

A marble of radius $R$ and mass $m$ is dropped from rest in olive oil of viscosity $\eta$. Find its terminal speed using Stokes' law $k = 6\pi\eta R$. (From [2], Example 8.5.)

  1. $v_\infty = mg/k = \dfrac{mg}{6\pi\eta R}$.

  2. For $m = 10\ \text{g}$, $R = 5\ \text{mm}$, $\eta = 0.081\ \text{kg/(m·s)}$ (olive oil at 25 °C): $v_\infty \approx \dfrac{(0.010)(9.81)}{6\pi(0.081)(0.005)} \approx 1.3\ \text{m/s}$.

Check

A heavier marble (same size) falls faster; a more viscous fluid slows it down. ✓

Example

A $75\ \text{kg}$ skydiver, cross-section $A \approx 0.7\ \text{m}^2$, drag coefficient $C \approx 1.0$, falls through air of density $\rho = 1.2\ \text{kg/m}^3$. Find the terminal speed. (Adapted from [2], Example 8.6.)

  1. Quadratic drag balance: $mg = \tfrac12 C\rho A v_\infty^2$, so $v_\infty = \sqrt{\dfrac{2mg}{C\rho A}}$.

  2. Insert numbers: $v_\infty = \sqrt{\dfrac{2(75)(9.81)}{(1.0)(1.2)(0.7)}} \approx 42\ \text{m/s}$, about 150 km/h.

Check

Spreading your arms doubles $A$, dropping $v_\infty$ by $\sqrt2 \approx 41\%$. Pulling in halves it, raising $v_\infty$ by $\sqrt2$ ✓.

Example

A block of mass $m$ on a horizontal surface is pushed with a horizontal force $F$. The coefficients of static and kinetic friction are $\mu_s$ and $\mu_k$. What is the smallest $F$ that makes the block move, and what is its acceleration once it does? (Based on [2], §8.3.2.)

  1. Normal force: $N = mg$. Static friction can match any applied force up to $\mu_s mg$.

  2. Minimum force to start moving: $F_{\min} = \mu_s mg$.

  3. Once sliding, kinetic friction $\mu_k mg$ opposes the motion: $a = (F - \mu_k mg)/m = F/m - \mu_k g$.

Check

Since $\mu_k < \mu_s$, as soon as the block breaks loose the opposing friction drops: it accelerates even if $F$ is held constant at $F_{\min}$. That's why pushing furniture is easier once it starts moving ✓.
