Momentum, F = dp/dt = ma, Galilean Invariance

The second law as an equation of motion: force equals the rate of change of momentum, F = ma for constant mass, and why it looks the same to every inertial observer.

In short

Momentum is "amount of motion": mass times velocity, $\mathbf p = m\mathbf v$ [1].

Force is what changes it. The stronger the force, the faster the momentum changes [1]; [2]:

$$\mathbf F = \frac{d\mathbf p}{dt}.$$

A short push gives a short kick: force × time = change in momentum, $\mathbf F\,\Delta t = \Delta\mathbf p$ [2].

For things whose mass doesn't change (almost everything in this course), this becomes $\mathbf F = m\mathbf a$. Push twice as hard and it speeds up twice as quickly; make it twice as heavy and it speeds up half as quickly [1]; [2].

The equation of motion. If you know the force, $m\ddot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf F$ is a "riddle" for the path $\mathbf r(t)$. Solve it, plug in where the object started and how fast it was going, and you know its whole future [1]; [2].

Everyone agrees on forces. A friend gliding past you at a steady speed sees different positions and velocities, but the same acceleration, so the same force. The laws are the same for both of you. That's Galilean invariance [1].

Theory

Force as rate of change of momentum

With momentum $\mathbf p = m\mathbf v$, Newton's second axiom says that a net force is what changes momentum [1, §Newton's Axioms of Motion]; [2, §7.3]:

$$\mathbf F = \frac{d\mathbf p}{dt}.$$

Newton first stated it for a short push: a force acting for a time $\Delta t$ produces an impulse $\mathbf I = \mathbf F\Delta t = \Delta\mathbf p$. Letting $\Delta t \to 0$ gives the instantaneous form $\mathbf F = \lim_{\Delta t\to0}\Delta\mathbf p/\Delta t = d\mathbf p/dt$ [2, §7.3].

Constant mass: F = ma

If the mass does not change, $d\mathbf p/dt = m\,d\mathbf v/dt$, so [1]; [2, §7.3]

$$\mathbf F = m\mathbf a, \qquad \mathbf a = \frac{\mathbf F}{m}.$$

The acceleration is proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to the mass, and it points in the direction of the force [1].

The equation of motion

Once the force is known as a function of position, velocity and time, the second law becomes a differential equation for the motion, the equation of motion [1, §Equations of Motion]; [2, §7.3]:

$$m\ddot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf F(\mathbf r, \dot{\mathbf r}, t).$$

It is second order, so its solution is fixed by two vector initial conditions: the initial position and the initial velocity [1].

When the force depends on position, it helps to use the chain rule to write $a = \dfrac{dv}{dt} = \dfrac{dv}{dx}\dfrac{dx}{dt} = v\dfrac{dv}{dx}$, which links speed and position directly [1, Worked Example “Force law from a given speed function”].

Galilean invariance

Let $S'$ move at constant velocity $\mathbf v_0$ relative to the inertial frame $S$. Then [1, §Galilean Invariance and Inertial Frames]

$$\begin{gathered}\mathbf r' = \mathbf r - \mathbf v_0t, \qquad \mathbf v' = \mathbf v - \mathbf v_0, \\ \mathbf a' = \mathbf a, \qquad t' = t.\end{gathered}$$

Positions, velocities and momenta differ between the frames, but accelerations agree. Therefore $\mathbf F' = m\mathbf a' = m\mathbf a = \mathbf F$: the second law has the same form in every inertial frame. This is Galilean invariance [1].

Note

Momentum depends on the frame (it contains $\mathbf v$), but its rate of change does not, as long as the frames move at constant velocity relative to each other: $d\mathbf p'/dt = m\mathbf a' = m\mathbf a = d\mathbf p/dt$ [1]; [3, §10.2].

Warning

$\mathbf F = m\mathbf a$ assumes the mass is constant. The general statement is $\mathbf F = d\mathbf p/dt$, since both mass and velocity can change with time [1, §Newton's Axioms of Motion]; [2, §7.3]. Also, $\mathbf F$ is the net force: add all forces as vectors before using it.

Visualization

A constant force $F$ pushes a block of mass $m$ for 3 s, then stops. The graph shows the momentum $p(t)$: while the force acts, $p$ grows in a straight line with slope $dp/dt = F$; after the force stops, the slope is zero and $p$ stays constant (first law). The velocity is $v = p/m$. Change $m$: the momentum graph is identical, because it depends only on the force, but a heavier block ends up slower.

Worked examples

Example

A particle of mass $m$ moves along a line with speed $v(x) = \alpha/x$, where $\alpha > 0$. Find the force acting on it as a function of $x$. (From [1], Worked Example “Force law from a given speed function”.)

  1. Use $a = v\,dv/dx$, since $v$ is given as a function of position.

  2. $\dfrac{dv}{dx} = -\dfrac{\alpha}{x^2}$, so $a = \dfrac{\alpha}{x}\cdot\left(-\dfrac{\alpha}{x^2}\right) = -\dfrac{\alpha^2}{x^3}$.

  3. $F = ma = -\dfrac{m\alpha^2}{x^3}$.

Check

The particle slows down as $x$ grows ($v = \alpha/x$ decreases), so for $x > 0$ the force must point backward: $F < 0$. ✓

Example

A block slides from rest down a frictionless incline of angle $\theta$, with equation of motion $\ddot x = g\sin\theta$ along the incline. Find its speed after a distance $x$ without solving for $x(t)$. (From [1], Worked Example “Velocity with respect to distance”, Solution 1.)

  1. Multiply the equation of motion by $2\dot x$: $2\dot x\ddot x = 2g\sin\theta\,\dot x$.

  2. The left side is $\frac{d}{dt}(\dot x^2)$ and the right side is $\frac{d}{dt}(2g\sin\theta\,x)$, so $\dot x^2 = 2g\sin\theta\,x + C$.

  3. Starting from rest at $x = 0$ gives $C = 0$, so $\dot x = \sqrt{2g\sin\theta\,x}$.

Check

The same result follows from energy conservation: $mgx\sin\theta = \tfrac12m\dot x^2$ [1, Solution 2]. ✓

Example

Show that an observer moving at constant velocity $\mathbf v_0$ measures the same force on a particle as an observer at rest in an inertial frame. (From [1], §Galilean Invariance and Inertial Frames.)

  1. With origins coinciding at $t = 0$: $\mathbf r' = \mathbf r - \mathbf v_0t$.

  2. Differentiate: $\mathbf v' = \mathbf v - \mathbf v_0$; again, since $\mathbf v_0$ is constant: $\mathbf a' = \mathbf a$.

  3. Hence $\mathbf F' = m\mathbf a' = m\mathbf a = \mathbf F$.

Check

If $\mathbf v_0$ were changing, an extra term $-m\,d\mathbf v_0/dt$ would appear: that is exactly the fictitious force of a non-inertial frame [4, §31.2]. ✓
