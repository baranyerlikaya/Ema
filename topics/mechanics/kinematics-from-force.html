Full Kinematics from F(t): a(t), v(t), x(t), Power

Starting from a time-dependent force F(t), find the acceleration, velocity and position by integrating twice, and compute the power delivered by the force.

In short

If you know the force at every moment, you know the whole motion. That's the whole story. Just divide by mass, then "add things up" (integrate) twice [1]; [2].

The three-step recipe:

  1. Acceleration: $a(t) = F(t)/m$. Divide out the mass.
  2. Velocity: add up acceleration over time (integrate). Start with $v_0$: $v(t) = v_0 + \int_0^t a\,dt'$. Graphically, it's the area under the acceleration curve [2].
  3. Position: add up velocity. Start with $x_0$: $x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t v\,dt'$.

Each step needs one starting fact. That's why second-order motion needs two: initial position and initial velocity [1].

Impulse = force × time. A short, hard kick and a long, gentle push can give the same change in momentum: $J = \int F\,dt = \Delta p$. Soccer, airbags, catching a ball: it's all impulse [1]; [3].

Power = force × velocity. How fast energy flows into the thing you're pushing: $P = F\,v$ (or more generally, $P = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v$) [1].

  • Push a cart twice as fast with the same force: twice the power.
  • Climb stairs faster: more power, same energy.

Units: 1 watt = 1 joule per second. Your body sustains about 100 W; a sprinter can briefly produce over 1 kW. A 200 hp car engine is 147 kW, about 1500 of you [1].

Theory

From force to acceleration

For a particle of constant mass, Newton's second law reads [1, §Newton's Axioms of Motion]

$$\mathbf a(t) = \frac{\mathbf F(t)}{m}.$$

If $\mathbf F$ depends only on time (not position or velocity), $\mathbf a$ is known as an explicit function of time, so velocity and position follow by direct integration.

From a(t) to v(t) to x(t)

Integrate the acceleration once to get velocity, and the velocity to get position, using the initial conditions $v(0) = v_0$ and $x(0) = x_0$ [1, §Equations of Motion]; [2, §4.6]:

$$\begin{gathered}v(t) = v_0 + \int_0^t a(t')\,dt' \\ x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t v(t')\,dt'.\end{gathered}$$

Each integration adds a constant of integration, fixed by the initial condition. In 2D or 3D, do this component by component.

Impulse and the final momentum

Integrating $m\dot v = F(t)$ over an interval gives the impulse, equal to the change in momentum [1, §Impulse and Power]; [3, §10.2]:

$$\mathbf J = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\mathbf F(t)\,dt = \mathbf p(t_2) - \mathbf p(t_1).$$

Geometrically, $|\mathbf J|$ is the area under the $|F|$-vs-$t$ graph. The average force over the interval is $\mathbf F_{\text{avg}} = \mathbf J/\Delta t$ [1].

Power

The instantaneous power delivered by the force to the particle is the rate at which it does work [1, §Impulse and Power]:

$$P(t) = \frac{dW}{dt} = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v.$$

$P > 0$ when the force and velocity point the same way (energy flows into the particle), and $P < 0$ when they oppose each other (energy leaves). Over an interval, the total work is $W = \int P\,dt$ [1].

Units: joule per second = watt (W). The horsepower comes from history: $1\ \text{hp} \approx 735\ \text{W}$ [1, Worked Example “Horsepower”].

Worked recipe

Given $F(t)$ along a line and the initial state, the full motion is [2, §4.6]:

  1. $a(t) = F(t)/m$;
  2. $v(t) = v_0 + \int_0^t a\,dt'$;
  3. $x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t v\,dt'$;
  4. Power: $P(t) = F(t)\,v(t)$.

Note

If the force depends on position or velocity rather than time, this direct integration fails and you have to solve a differential equation. The time-dependent case is special because $a(t)$ is given explicitly [1, §Equations of Motion].

Warning

Don't integrate $F(t)$ to get $v(t)$ directly; divide by $m$ first. The impulse $\int F\,dt$ gives $\Delta p = m\Delta v$, not $\Delta v$ [1]; [3, §10.2].

Worked examples

Example

A particle of mass $m$ at rest at $x = 0$ is pushed by $F(t) = k\cos(\omega t)$ along a frictionless $x$-axis. Find $a(t)$, $v(t)$, $x(t)$ and the power delivered at time $t$. (Applying the general recipe from [1], §Equations of Motion.)

  1. $a(t) = F(t)/m = (k/m)\cos(\omega t)$.

  2. $v(t) = \int_0^t a\,dt' = \dfrac{k}{m\omega}\sin(\omega t)$, using $v(0) = 0$.

  3. $x(t) = \int_0^t v\,dt' = \dfrac{k}{m\omega^2}\left[1 - \cos(\omega t)\right]$, using $x(0) = 0$.

  4. Power: $P(t) = F\,v = \dfrac{k^2}{m\omega}\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega t) = \dfrac{k^2}{2m\omega}\sin(2\omega t)$.

Check

Differentiate $v(t)$ once to recover $a(t)$ ✓, and twice to recover $(k/m)\cos\omega t = F/m$ ✓. The average power over a period is zero: the force gives energy back and forth. ✓

Example

Same setup with $F(t) = a + bt$. Find $a(t)$, $v(t)$, $x(t)$ and the power at time $t$. (Applying the general recipe from [1], §Equations of Motion.)

  1. $a_{\text{acc}}(t) = (a + bt)/m$.

  2. $v(t) = \dfrac{at}{m} + \dfrac{bt^2}{2m}$.

  3. $x(t) = \dfrac{at^2}{2m} + \dfrac{bt^3}{6m}$.

  4. $P(t) = F\,v = (a + bt)\left(\dfrac{at}{m} + \dfrac{bt^2}{2m}\right)$.

Check

Setting $b = 0$: $F = a$ constant, $v = at/m$, $x = at^2/(2m)$, matching the constant-force result [1, Worked Example “Motion of a Particle under a Constant Force”]. ✓

Example

A soccer ball of mass $0.5\ \text{kg}$ is kicked with a constant force of $200\ \text{N}$ for $0.1\ \text{s}$. Find the impulse and the ball's final speed. (From [1], Worked Example “Impulse — 1”.)

  1. Impulse: $J = F\Delta t = 200 \times 0.1 = 20\ \text{N·s}$.

  2. Change in momentum: $\Delta p = J = 20\ \text{kg·m/s}$.

  3. Starting from rest: $v = \Delta p / m = 20 / 0.5 = 40\ \text{m/s}$.

Check

Check with $F = ma$: $a = F/m = 400\ \text{m/s}^2$, so after $0.1\ \text{s}$, $v = at = 40\ \text{m/s}$. ✓

Example

A $70\ \text{kg}$ person climbs stairs of height $20\ \text{m}$ in $10\ \text{s}$. Find the average power output. Compare with a 200 hp car. (From [1], Worked Examples “Power” and “Horsepower”.)

  1. Work against gravity: $W = mgh = 70 \times 9.8 \times 20 \approx 13.7\ \text{kJ}$.

  2. Average power: $P = W/\Delta t = 13.7\ \text{kJ}/10\ \text{s} = 1.37\ \text{kW}$, or about $1.9\ \text{hp}$.

  3. 200 hp $= 200 \times 735\ \text{W} \approx 147\ \text{kW}$: about 100 times the person's power.

Check

The climb takes only 10 s and 13.7 kJ. A car engine at full throttle delivers that same energy in less than 0.1 s ✓.
