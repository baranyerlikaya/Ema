Two Particles: Closest Approach

Two particles moving in straight lines at constant velocity: when are they closest, how close do they get, and do they collide? Solved with the separation vector and one derivative.

In short

Two boats on a lake, each going straight at a steady speed. Will they crash? If not, how close will they get, and when?

Step 1: look at the gap. Draw an arrow from boat 1 to boat 2. That's the separation vector $\mathbf R = \mathbf r_2 - \mathbf r_1$. Its length is how far apart they are [1].

Step 2: pretend you're on boat 1. From there, boat 2 seems to move with the relative velocity $\mathbf V = \mathbf v_2 - \mathbf v_1$, in a straight line: $\mathbf R(t) = \mathbf R_0 + \mathbf V t$ [3].

Step 3: find the closest moment. The distance shrinks, reaches a smallest value, then grows again. At the smallest point it stops shrinking, so its derivative is zero. It's easier to use the distance squared; that gives [2]

$$t_c = -\frac{\mathbf R_0\cdot\mathbf V}{|\mathbf V|^2}, \qquad d_{\min} = |\mathbf R_0 + \mathbf V t_c|.$$

At that moment the gap arrow is exactly perpendicular to the relative velocity, like the shortest line from you to a passing road [2].

Crash or not? They collide only if the gap becomes exactly zero: same $x$ and same $y$ at the same time. Paths crossing isn't enough; they have to be there at the same moment [1].

If $t_c$ is negative, the closest moment was in the past and they are already moving apart.

Theory

The separation vector

For two particles with positions $\mathbf r_1(t)$ and $\mathbf r_2(t)$, define the separation (relative position) vector [1, Worked Example “Collision of Two Particles”]

$$\mathbf R(t) = \mathbf r_2(t) - \mathbf r_1(t).$$

It points from particle 1 to particle 2, and its length $|\mathbf R(t)|$ is the distance between them at time $t$. Its derivative is the relative velocity $\mathbf V = \dot{\mathbf R} = \mathbf v_2 - \mathbf v_1$: the velocity of particle 2 as seen by an observer riding on particle 1 [3, §11.3].

Collision

The particles collide when they are at the same place at the same time, i.e. $\mathbf R(t) = \mathbf 0$. Every component must vanish at one common instant [1]:

$$\begin{gathered}x_2(t) - x_1(t) = 0, \\ y_2(t) - y_1(t) = 0 \quad (\text{and the same for } z).\end{gathered}$$

Equal $x$ at one time and equal $y$ at a different time is not a collision: the paths cross, but the particles pass the crossing point at different times [1, Worked Example “Displacement, Average Velocity, Instantaneous Velocity, and Collision”].

Uniform motion: the relative motion is a straight line

If both particles move with constant velocities, $\mathbf r_i(t) = \mathbf r_{i0} + \mathbf v_i t$, then

$$\begin{gathered}\mathbf R(t) = \mathbf R_0 + \mathbf V t, \\ \mathbf R_0 = \mathbf r_{20} - \mathbf r_{10}, \qquad \mathbf V = \mathbf v_2 - \mathbf v_1.\end{gathered}$$

Seen from particle 1, particle 2 moves along a straight line with constant velocity $\mathbf V$. So the closest approach is the point of that line nearest to particle 1 [3, §11.3].

Finding the moment of closest approach

Minimizing the distance is easier through its square, which has its minimum at the same time [2, §Maxima and Minima of Functions]:

$$\begin{aligned}D^2(t) &= |\mathbf R|^2 = \mathbf R\cdot\mathbf R \\ &= |\mathbf R_0|^2 + 2(\mathbf R_0\cdot\mathbf V)\,t + |\mathbf V|^2 t^2.\end{aligned}$$

This is a quadratic in $t$. Set its derivative to zero, $\frac{d}{dt}D^2 = 2\,\mathbf R\cdot\mathbf V = 0$:

$$t_c = -\frac{\mathbf R_0\cdot\mathbf V}{|\mathbf V|^2}, \qquad d_{\min} = |\mathbf R_0 + \mathbf V t_c|.$$

Since $|\mathbf V|^2 > 0$, this stationary point is a minimum ($\frac{d^2}{dt^2}D^2 = 2|\mathbf V|^2 > 0$) [2]. Geometrically, $\mathbf R\cdot\mathbf V = 0$ says that at closest approach the separation is perpendicular to the relative velocity [2, §Dot Product].

Equivalently, $d_{\min}$ is the distance from a point to a line, which the cross product gives directly [2, §Cross Product]:

$$d_{\min} = \frac{|\mathbf R_0 \times \mathbf V|}{|\mathbf V|}.$$

The particles collide exactly when $d_{\min} = 0$.

Note

If $t_c$ comes out negative, the closest approach happened before $t = 0$: the particles are already moving apart. If only $t \ge 0$ matters, the closest distance is the initial one, $|\mathbf R_0|$. If $\mathbf V = \mathbf 0$, the particles move together and their distance never changes.

Warning

Minimize the distance between the particles, $|\mathbf r_2 - \mathbf r_1|$, not the distance of each particle from the origin, and use the same $t$ for both. Finding where the two paths cross does not tell you where the particles are closest [1].

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

Two particles move as $\mathbf r_1(t) = t\,\hat\imath + t\,\hat\jmath$ and $\mathbf r_2(t) = (4 - t)\,\hat\imath + (2t - 2)\,\hat\jmath$. Find their closest approach. Do they collide? (From [1], Worked Example “Collision of Two Particles”, Solution 2.)

  1. Separation: $\mathbf R = \mathbf r_2 - \mathbf r_1 = (4 - 2t)\,\hat\imath + (t - 2)\,\hat\jmath$, so $\mathbf R_0 = 4\hat\imath - 2\hat\jmath$ and $\mathbf V = -2\hat\imath + \hat\jmath$.

  2. Squared distance: $D^2 = (4-2t)^2 + (t-2)^2 = 5t^2 - 20t + 20 = 5(t-2)^2$.

  3. The minimum is at $t_c = 2$, where $D = 0$: they collide, at $\mathbf r_1(2) = 2\hat\imath + 2\hat\jmath$.

Check

Formula: $\mathbf R_0\cdot\mathbf V = -8 - 2 = -10$ and $|\mathbf V|^2 = 5$, so $t_c = 10/5 = 2$ ✓. Also $\mathbf r_2(2) = 2\hat\imath + 2\hat\jmath = \mathbf r_1(2)$ ✓.

Example

Same as Example 1, but particle 2 starts one unit higher: $\mathbf r_2(t) = (4 - t)\,\hat\imath + (2t - 1)\,\hat\jmath$. Find the time and distance of closest approach. (Adapted from [1], Worked Example “Collision of Two Particles”.)

  1. $\mathbf R_0 = 4\hat\imath - \hat\jmath$ and $\mathbf V = -2\hat\imath + \hat\jmath$ (the velocities haven't changed).

  2. $\mathbf R_0\cdot\mathbf V = -8 - 1 = -9$ and $|\mathbf V|^2 = 5$, so $t_c = 9/5 = 1.8$.

  3. $\mathbf R(1.8) = (4 - 3.6)\,\hat\imath + (1.8 - 1)\,\hat\jmath = 0.4\,\hat\imath + 0.8\,\hat\jmath$, so $d_{\min} = \sqrt{0.16 + 0.64} = \sqrt{0.8} \approx 0.89$.

Check

Cross-product formula: $|\mathbf R_0\times\mathbf V| = |4\cdot1 - (-1)(-2)| = 2$, so $d_{\min} = 2/\sqrt5 \approx 0.89$ ✓. And $\mathbf R(t_c)\cdot\mathbf V = -0.8 + 0.8 = 0$: perpendicular, as it must be ✓.

Example

Two particles move as $\mathbf r_1 = (t^2+1)\,\hat\imath + 2t\,\hat\jmath$ and $\mathbf r_2 = (5-t)\,\hat\imath + (t^2-1)\,\hat\jmath$. Do they collide? (From [1], Worked Example “Displacement, Average Velocity, Instantaneous Velocity, and Collision”, (e).)

  1. Equal $x$: $t^2 + 1 = 5 - t$, i.e. $t^2 + t - 4 = 0$, so $t = \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt{17}}{2}$ (positive root $\approx 1.56$).

  2. Equal $y$: $2t = t^2 - 1$, i.e. $t^2 - 2t - 1 = 0$, so $t = 1 \pm \sqrt2$ (positive root $\approx 2.41$).

  3. No single time satisfies both, so the particles never collide.

Check

The $x$-coordinates match at one moment and the $y$-coordinates at another: the paths come close, but the particles are never in the same place at the same time [1]. ✓
