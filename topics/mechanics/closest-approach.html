Two Particles: Closest Approach

How close two interacting particles get: reduce the pair to one particle of reduced mass, then find where the radial velocity vanishes in the effective potential.

Theory

Reduction to a one-body problem

Take two particles of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ that interact only with each other, through a potential $U(r)$ that depends only on their separation $r = |\vec r_2 - \vec r_1|$. With no external force, the centre of mass $\vec R = (m_1\vec r_1 + m_2\vec r_2)/M$, where $M = m_1 + m_2$, moves with constant velocity and plays no further role.

Everything interesting is in the relative coordinate $\vec r = \vec r_2 - \vec r_1$. Rewriting the kinetic energy in terms of $\vec R$ and $\vec r$ splits it into a centre-of-mass part and a relative part:

$$T = \tfrac12 M\dot{\vec R}^{\,2} + \tfrac12 \mu\,\dot{\vec r}^{\,2}, \qquad \mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}.$$

So the relative motion is that of a single fictitious particle of mass $\mu$, the reduced mass, moving in the fixed potential $U(r)$. The separation of the two real particles is exactly the distance of this fictitious particle from the origin, so the closest approach of the pair is the minimum of $r$ for the one-body problem [2, §3.1.1].

Effective potential and the centrifugal barrier

Because the force is central, the relative angular momentum $\vec L = \mu\,\vec r \times \dot{\vec r}$ is conserved. The motion therefore stays in a fixed plane, and in polar coordinates $(r, \varphi)$ in that plane $L = \mu r^2 \dot\varphi$ [2, §3.1.2].

Using $\dot\varphi = L/(\mu r^2)$, the energy of the relative motion becomes a function of $r$ and $\dot r$ only:

$$E = \tfrac12 \mu \dot r^2 + \underbrace{U(r) + \frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}}_{U_{\text{eff}}(r)}.$$

The radial motion is then one-dimensional motion in the effective potential $U_{\text{eff}}$. The extra term $L^2/(2\mu r^2)$ is the centrifugal barrier: it grows without bound as $r \to 0$, so a particle with $L \neq 0$ cannot reach $r = 0$ unless $U$ is very strongly attractive there [2, §3.1.2, §3.2].

Turning points

Since $\tfrac12\mu\dot r^2 \ge 0$, the motion is only possible where $E \ge U_{\text{eff}}(r)$. Solving the energy equation for the radial speed gives

$$\dot r = \pm\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\mu}\bigl(E - U_{\text{eff}}(r)\bigr)}.$$

An incoming particle has $\dot r < 0$. It keeps approaching until it reaches the smallest $r$ with $E = U_{\text{eff}}(r)$. There $\dot r = 0$, the radial motion reverses, and $r$ starts to grow again. This radius is a turning point, and for a scattering (unbound) motion it is the distance of closest approach $r_{\min}$ [2, §3.2]; [1, §20.2].

At $r_{\min}$ the velocity is not zero in general; only its radial component vanishes. The velocity is perpendicular to $\vec r$ there, so $L = \mu v_{\min} r_{\min}$ [1, §20.2].

$r_{\min}$ from the initial conditions

Suppose the particles start far apart ($r \to \infty$, where $U \to 0$) with relative speed $v_0$, and the line of the initial relative velocity misses the other particle by a perpendicular distance $b$, the impact parameter. Then both conserved quantities are fixed by $v_0$ and $b$ [2, §3.5]:

$$E = \tfrac12 \mu v_0^2, \qquad L = \mu v_0 b.$$

Substituting these into the turning-point condition $E = U_{\text{eff}}(r_{\min})$ gives

$$\tfrac12 \mu v_0^2 = U(r_{\min}) + \frac{\tfrac12 \mu v_0^2\, b^2}{r_{\min}^2}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad 1 - \frac{b^2}{r_{\min}^2} = \frac{U(r_{\min})}{\tfrac12\mu v_0^2}.$$

Solve this for $r_{\min}$ and take the largest root, which is the first one the incoming particle meets. For $U = 0$ it gives $r_{\min} = b$: a free particle passes at exactly the impact parameter. A repulsive $U$ pushes $r_{\min}$ above $b$, and an attractive $U$ pulls it below.

When one mass is much larger, $\mu$ is close to the lighter mass and the heavy particle hardly moves. This is the fixed-target case treated in [1, §20.2–20.4].

Note

$v_0$ is the relative speed $|\dot{\vec r}_2 - \dot{\vec r}_1|$ at large separation, and $E$ is the energy of the relative motion only. The centre-of-mass kinetic energy $\tfrac12 M \dot{\vec R}^{\,2}$ is conserved separately and cannot be spent on getting closer.

Warning

Don't set the whole velocity to zero at closest approach. Only $\dot r = 0$. Unless $b = 0$, the particles still move sideways at $r_{\min}$, and that tangential motion carries the angular momentum $L$.

Worked examples

Example

Two particles with like charges $q_1$, $q_2$ and masses $m_1$, $m_2$ approach each other from far away with relative speed $v_0$ and impact parameter $b$. Find their distance of closest approach. (Adapted from [1, §20.2 and §20.4], with the fixed target replaced by the reduced mass from [2].)

  1. Reduce to one body. The relative motion is a particle of mass $\mu = m_1 m_2/(m_1+m_2)$ in $U(r) = k/r$ with $k = q_1 q_2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) > 0$. From the initial conditions, $E = \tfrac12\mu v_0^2$ and $L = \mu v_0 b$.

  2. Apply the turning-point condition $\dot r = 0$:

    $$E = \frac{L^2}{2\mu r_{\min}^2} + \frac{k}{r_{\min}}.$$

    Multiply by $r_{\min}^2$ to get a quadratic:

    $$E\, r_{\min}^2 - k\, r_{\min} - \frac{L^2}{2\mu} = 0.$$

  3. Solve the quadratic and keep the positive root:

    $$r_{\min} = \frac{k}{2E}\left[1 + \sqrt{1 + \frac{2EL^2}{\mu k^2}}\,\right].$$

  4. Insert $E$ and $L$. Since $\dfrac{2EL^2}{\mu k^2} = \left(\dfrac{\mu v_0^2 b}{k}\right)^2$,

    $$r_{\min} = \frac{k}{\mu v_0^2}\left[1 + \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\mu v_0^2\, b}{k}\right)^{2}}\,\right].$$

Check

For $k \to 0$ (no force), $\sqrt{1 + (\mu v_0^2 b/k)^2} \approx \mu v_0^2 b/k$, so $r_{\min} \to b$, as for a straight line. For $b = 0$ the bracket is $2$ and $r_{\min} = 2k/(\mu v_0^2)$. In every case $r_{\min} > b$, as it must be for a repulsive force.

Example

An alpha particle ($z = 2$) with kinetic energy $K = 7.7\ \text{MeV}$ is fired head-on ($b = 0$) at a gold nucleus ($Z = 79$). How close does it get? Use $e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 1.44\ \text{MeV fm}$. (Adapted from [3], which treats the gold nucleus as fixed; the recoil correction uses [2].)

  1. With $b = 0$ we have $L = 0$, so there is no centrifugal barrier. The particle stops completely at $r_{\min}$, and all of the relative kinetic energy has turned into Coulomb energy: $E = k/r_{\min}$.

  2. Here $k = zZ\,e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 2 \times 79 \times 1.44\ \text{MeV fm} = 227.5\ \text{MeV fm}$.

  3. Fixed nucleus ($\mu \approx m_\alpha$, so $E \approx K$):

    $$r_{\min} = \frac{k}{K} = \frac{227.5}{7.7}\ \text{fm} \approx 29.5\ \text{fm}.$$

  4. With recoil: the gold nucleus starts at rest, so $v_0$ is the alpha's speed and $E = \tfrac12\mu v_0^2 = K\,\dfrac{m_{\text{Au}}}{m_\alpha + m_{\text{Au}}}$. Using mass numbers $4$ and $197$, $E \approx 7.7 \times \tfrac{197}{201} \approx 7.55\ \text{MeV}$, so $r_{\min} \approx 30.1\ \text{fm}$.

Check

The recoil changes the answer by only about $2\%$, because gold is about 50 times heavier than the alpha particle. For two equal masses $\mu = m/2$, so only half of the lab kinetic energy is available and $r_{\min}$ doubles compared with the fixed-target estimate.
