Superposition of Motions

Complicated motions built by adding simple ones: a projectile as sideways plus vertical motion, a helix as circle plus straight line, a rolling wheel as sliding plus spinning.

In short

Big motions are made of small ones. Many motions that look complicated are just two simple motions happening at the same time. Add the positions and you get the real path. Velocities and accelerations simply add too [1]; [2].

Throwing a ball = sliding sideways at a steady speed plus going up and down like a ball tossed straight up. Each part follows its own simple rule [3]; [4].

A spiral staircase path (helix) = going round in a circle plus going steadily up [2].

A circle = a back-and-forth swing left–right plus a back-and-forth swing up–down, a quarter-swing apart [2].

A rolling wheel. Put a dot of paint on a bicycle tyre and roll it. The dot traces a curve of arches called a cycloid [2]. Its motion is the wheel sliding forward plus the dot spinning around the centre. At the bottom the two cancel, so the dot touching the ground is momentarily still. At the top they add up, so the dot moves twice as fast as the bike.

Moving floors. Walking on a moving walkway: your speed relative to the ground = your walking speed + the walkway's speed, added as arrows [5]; [4].

Always add as arrows (vectors), at the same moment, never just the sizes.

Theory

The idea

If a position can be written as a sum of simpler motions, $\mathbf r(t) = \mathbf r_A(t) + \mathbf r_B(t)$, then because the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives [1, §Derivatives of Common Functions],

$$\mathbf v = \mathbf v_A + \mathbf v_B, \qquad \mathbf a = \mathbf a_A + \mathbf a_B.$$

So you can study each piece on its own and add the results. The same linearity holds for integration, component by component [2, §Connecting position, velocity, and acceleration].

Independent directions

The most common split is into perpendicular directions. Each component of the motion obeys its own one-dimensional law [3, §5.1]. For a projectile, the motion "can be divided into horizontal and vertical components": uniform motion sideways plus uniformly accelerated motion vertically [4, Worked Example “Projectile Motion”]; [3, §5.2]:

$$\mathbf r(t) = \underbrace{(v_0\cos\theta)\,t\,\hat\imath}_{\text{uniform}} + \underbrace{\left(v_0\sin\theta\,t - \tfrac12gt^2\right)\hat\jmath}_{\text{free fall}}.$$

Circle plus line, oscillation plus oscillation

Spiral (helical) motion is circular motion in the $xy$-plane combined with linear motion along $z$ [2, §Spiral Motion]:

$$\mathbf r(t) = R\cos\theta(t)\,\hat\imath + R\sin\theta(t)\,\hat\jmath + z(t)\,\hat k.$$

Conversely, two perpendicular simple harmonic oscillations of equal amplitude and frequency, $x = r\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ and $y = r\sin(\omega t + \phi)$, add up to uniform circular motion [2, §Oscillatory Motion].

Rolling: translation plus rotation

A point on the rim of a wheel of radius $R$ rolling without slipping traces a cycloid [2, §Cycloidal Motion]:

$$\mathbf r = R(\phi - \sin\phi)\,\hat\imath + R(1 - \cos\phi)\,\hat\jmath,$$

where $\phi$ is the angle the wheel has turned. This is the sum of two simple motions: the centre slides forward, $R\phi\,\hat\imath + R\,\hat\jmath$, and the point turns around the centre, $-R\sin\phi\,\hat\imath - R\cos\phi\,\hat\jmath$.

Moving frames

Motion seen from a moving frame is also a superposition. If a body has velocity $\mathbf v'$ in a frame that itself moves at $\mathbf V$, its velocity relative to the ground is the sum $\mathbf v = \mathbf v' + \mathbf V$ [5, §11.3]; [4, §Galilean Invariance and Inertial Frames].

Note

Superposition of motions is pure kinematics: you can always add position vectors. Whether the pieces are physically independent depends on the forces. For a projectile they are, because gravity acts only vertically [3, §5.2].

Warning

Add the pieces as vectors, at the same time $t$. Speeds don't add as numbers: a point on a rolling wheel has speed $0$ at the bottom and $2v_{\text{centre}}$ at the top, even though the centre's speed and the rim's turning speed are equal in size (Example 2).

Visualization

A wheel rolls to the right without slipping, and a point on its rim draws a cycloid. At the point, the two grey arrows are the two motions being added: the forward motion of the centre and the turning motion around the centre, both of size $\omega R$. The dark arrow is their sum, the real velocity. At the bottom the two cancel and the point is momentarily at rest; at the top they add to $2\omega R$. The label shows the speed in units of $\omega R$.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

Show that a projectile launched from the origin with speed $v_0$ at angle $\theta$ is the sum of a uniform horizontal motion and a free fall, and write $\mathbf v(t)$. (From [4], Worked Example “Projectile Motion”.)

  1. Horizontal: no force, so $x = v_0\cos\theta\,t$ and $v_x = v_0\cos\theta$, a uniform motion.

  2. Vertical: constant acceleration $-g$, so $y = v_0\sin\theta\,t - \tfrac12gt^2$ and $v_y = v_0\sin\theta - gt$, a vertical throw.

  3. Add: $\mathbf v(t) = v_0\cos\theta\,\hat\imath + (v_0\sin\theta - gt)\,\hat\jmath$.

Check

$\mathbf a = \dot{\mathbf v} = -g\,\hat\jmath$: only the vertical piece accelerates. ✓

Example

A wheel of radius $R$ rolls without slipping, and a rim point follows $\mathbf r = R(\phi - \sin\phi)\,\hat\imath + R(1-\cos\phi)\,\hat\jmath$. Find the point's velocity when the wheel turns at a steady rate, $\phi = \omega t$, and its speed at the bottom and the top. (Adapted from [2], §Cycloidal Motion.)

  1. With $\dot\phi = \omega$: $\mathbf v = R\omega(1 - \cos\phi)\,\hat\imath + R\omega\sin\phi\,\hat\jmath$.

  2. Split: the centre moves at $R\omega\,\hat\imath$; the turning part is $-R\omega\cos\phi\,\hat\imath + R\omega\sin\phi\,\hat\jmath$. Their sum is the result above.

  3. Bottom ($\phi = 0$): $\mathbf v = 0$, the contact point is momentarily at rest. Top ($\phi = \pi$): $\mathbf v = 2R\omega\,\hat\imath$, twice the speed of the centre.

Check

Speed: $|\mathbf v| = R\omega\sqrt{(1-\cos\phi)^2 + \sin^2\phi} = R\omega\sqrt{2 - 2\cos\phi} = 2R\omega|\sin(\phi/2)|$, which is $0$ at $\phi = 0$ and $2R\omega$ at $\phi = \pi$. ✓

Example

A particle moves on the helix $\mathbf r = R\cos(\omega t)\,\hat\imath + R\sin(\omega t)\,\hat\jmath + ut\,\hat k$. Find its speed and acceleration. (Adapted from [2], §Spiral Motion.)

  1. $\mathbf v = -R\omega\sin(\omega t)\,\hat\imath + R\omega\cos(\omega t)\,\hat\jmath + u\,\hat k$: circular velocity plus a constant upward velocity.

  2. Speed: $v = \sqrt{R^2\omega^2 + u^2}$, constant.

  3. $\mathbf a = -R\omega^2\left(\cos\omega t\,\hat\imath + \sin\omega t\,\hat\jmath\right)$: only the circular part accelerates, toward the axis, with size $R\omega^2$.

Check

Setting $u = 0$ gives uniform circular motion ($v = R\omega$); setting $\omega = 0$ gives uniform straight-line motion ($v = u$). ✓
