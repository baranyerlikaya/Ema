Position, Displacement, Velocity, Acceleration

The basic language of motion: where a particle is, how far it has moved, how fast it moves and how fast that changes. And how to go back from acceleration to position.

In short

Where is it? Pick a starting spot (the origin). The position vector $\mathbf r$ is an arrow from that spot to the object. We treat the object as a tiny dot, a particle, when its size doesn't matter [1].

How far did it get? The displacement $\Delta\mathbf r = \mathbf r_{\text{end}} - \mathbf r_{\text{start}}$ is an arrow from where you started to where you ended. Careful: it's not the same as the distance you walked. If you walk to the shop and back home, your displacement is zero, but your legs walked a lot [1]; [2].

How fast? Velocity is how quickly position changes, and it has a direction. Average velocity = displacement ÷ time. Instantaneous velocity is the same thing over a tiny, tiny time: the derivative, $\mathbf v = d\mathbf r/dt$. On a position–time graph it's the steepness [1]; [2]. Speed is just how big the velocity is, with no direction, so it's never negative [1].

Speeding up or slowing down? Acceleration is how quickly velocity changes: $\mathbf a = d\mathbf v/dt$. On a line, if $v$ and $a$ have the same sign you speed up; if they have opposite signs you slow down [1].

Going backwards: if you know the acceleration, add it up over time (integrate) to get the velocity, and add up the velocity to get the position. Each time you need one starting fact: the starting velocity, then the starting position [1]; [2]. With constant acceleration:

$$v = v_0 + at, \qquad x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac12at^2.$$

On a graph: the area under the velocity line is the displacement [2].

Theory

Particle and position vector

A particle is a body whose size and shape don't matter for the problem, so we can treat it as a point with a mass. A planet orbiting the Sun can be a particle; the Earth cannot be one when we study its tides [1, §Notion of a Particle].

Where the particle is at time $t$ is given by its position vector, drawn from the origin to the particle [1, §The Position Vector]; [2, §4.2.1]:

$$\begin{gathered}\mathbf r(t) = x(t)\,\hat\imath + y(t)\,\hat\jmath + z(t)\,\hat k, \\ |\mathbf r| = \sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}.\end{gathered}$$

The curve traced by $\mathbf r(t)$ is the trajectory. Eliminating $t$ between the components gives the trajectory equation, e.g. $y(x)$ [1, §The Trajectory of a Particle].

Displacement

If the particle goes from $\mathbf r(t_1)$ to $\mathbf r(t_2)$, its displacement is the change in position [1, §The Displacement Vector]; [2, §4.2.3]:

$$\Delta\mathbf r = \mathbf r(t_2) - \mathbf r(t_1).$$

It is an arrow from the start point to the end point. It depends only on those two points, not on the path. Its length is the straight-line distance between them, which in general is not the distance travelled along the path [1].

Average and instantaneous velocity

The average velocity over the interval $\Delta t = t_2 - t_1$ is the displacement per unit time; it points the same way as $\Delta\mathbf r$ [1]; [2, §4.3.1]:

$$\mathbf v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta\mathbf r}{\Delta t}.$$

Making the interval shorter and shorter gives the instantaneous velocity, the derivative of position [1, §The Instantaneous Velocity Vector]; [2, §4.3.3]:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf v(t) &= \lim_{\Delta t\to0}\frac{\Delta\mathbf r}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\mathbf r}{dt} \\ &= \dot x\,\hat\imath + \dot y\,\hat\jmath + \dot z\,\hat k.\end{aligned}$$

On an $x$–$t$ graph, $v_{\text{avg}}$ is the slope of the secant between two points and $v(t)$ is the slope of the tangent at one point [2, §4.3.3]. The speed is the magnitude $v = |\mathbf v|$; it is never negative [1].

Average and instantaneous acceleration

Acceleration measures how the velocity changes. In the same way [1, §The Average Acceleration Vector, §The Instantaneous Acceleration Vector]; [2, §4.4]:

$$\begin{gathered}\mathbf a_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta\mathbf v}{\Delta t}, \\ \mathbf a(t) = \frac{d\mathbf v}{dt} = \frac{d^2\mathbf r}{dt^2} = \ddot x\,\hat\imath + \ddot y\,\hat\jmath + \ddot z\,\hat k.\end{gathered}$$

It is the slope of the $v$–$t$ graph [2, §4.4.2].

In one dimension the vectors reduce to signed numbers $x$, $v = \dot x$, $a = \ddot x$; the sign gives the direction along the axis [1, §Linear Motion]. A particle speeds up when $v$ and $a$ have the same sign and slows down when they have opposite signs [1, Worked Example “Velocity, Acceleration, and the Interpretation of Signs”].

From acceleration back to position

Differentiation takes $\mathbf r \to \mathbf v \to \mathbf a$. Integration goes back, one step at a time, and each step needs one initial condition [1, §Connecting position, velocity, and acceleration]; [2, §4.6]:

$$\begin{gathered}v(t_2) = v(t_1) + \int_{t_1}^{t_2} a\,dt, \\ x(t_2) = x(t_1) + \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v\,dt.\end{gathered}$$

Graphically, the change in velocity is the area under the $a$–$t$ graph, and the displacement is the area under the $v$–$t$ graph [2, §4.5].

For constant acceleration this gives the familiar formulas [2, §4.4–4.5]; [3, Worked Example “Motion of a Particle under a Constant Force”]:

$$v(t) = v_0 + at, \qquad x(t) = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac12at^2.$$

Note

Average velocity is displacement divided by time. It equals the mean of the initial and final velocities, $(v_i + v_f)/2$, only when $v$ changes linearly in time, i.e. for constant acceleration [2, Example 4.2].

Warning

Displacement is not distance. If a particle moves forward and then back, its displacement can be small or zero while the distance travelled is large. To get the distance, split the motion at every point where $v = 0$ and add the absolute values [1, Worked Example “Velocity, Acceleration, and the Interpretation of Signs”]; [2, §4.3.1].

Worked examples

Example

A particle moves along the $x$-axis with $x(t) = t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t$. Find $v(t)$ and $a(t)$, when it is at rest, when it speeds up or slows down, and compare the distance travelled in $0 \le t \le 4$ with the displacement. (From [1], Worked Example “Velocity, Acceleration, and the Interpretation of Signs”.)

  1. $v = \dot x = 3t^2 - 12t + 9 = 3(t-1)(t-3)$ and $a = \dot v = 6(t-2)$.

  2. At rest when $v = 0$: $t = 1$ and $t = 3$. It moves in $+x$ for $t < 1$, in $-x$ for $1 < t < 3$, and in $+x$ again for $t > 3$.

  3. Compare signs of $v$ and $a$: slowing down for $0 < t < 1$ and $2 < t < 3$ (opposite signs), speeding up for $1 < t < 2$ and $t > 3$ (same signs).

  4. $x(0) = 0$, $x(1) = 4$, $x(3) = 0$, $x(4) = 4$. Distance $= 4 + 4 + 4 = 12$, but displacement $= x(4) - x(0) = 4$.

Check

The distance is three times the displacement because the particle turns around twice. ✓

Example

A particle has $a(t) = 6t - 4$, with $x(0) = 2$ and $v(0) = -3$. Find $v(t)$, $x(t)$, and where it turns around. (From [1], Worked Example “Reconstructing Position and Velocity from Acceleration”.)

  1. Integrate: $v = 3t^2 - 4t + C_1$; $v(0) = -3$ gives $v = 3t^2 - 4t - 3$.

  2. Integrate again: $x = t^3 - 2t^2 - 3t + C_2$; $x(0) = 2$ gives $x = t^3 - 2t^2 - 3t + 2$.

  3. It turns around where $v = 0$: $t = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{13}}{3}$. Only the positive root is in the future: $t = \frac{2+\sqrt{13}}{3} \approx 1.87\ \text{s}$, where $x \approx -4.06$.

Check

Differentiating $x(t)$ twice gives back $\dot x = 3t^2 - 4t - 3$ and $\ddot x = 6t - 4$. ✓

Example

A car starts from rest, accelerates uniformly over $100\ \text{m}$ to $20\ \text{m/s}$, then keeps that speed for $10\ \text{s}$. How long did it accelerate, with what acceleration, and what was its average velocity for the whole trip? (From [2], Example 4.4.)

  1. From rest: $x = \frac12at^2$ and $v = at$. Eliminating $a$ gives $x = \frac12vt$, so $t_1 = 2x/v = 2(100)/20 = 10\ \text{s}$.

  2. $a = v/t_1 = 20/10 = 2.0\ \text{m/s}^2$.

  3. In the next $10\ \text{s}$ it covers $20 \times 10 = 200\ \text{m}$. Total: $300\ \text{m}$ in $20\ \text{s}$, so $v_{\text{avg}} = 15\ \text{m/s}$.

Check

$v_{\text{avg}}$ lies between $0$ and $20\ \text{m/s}$, closer to $20$ because the car spends half its time at full speed. ✓

Example

When a light turns green, a car starts from rest with $a = 3.0\ \text{m/s}^2$. At that instant a bus passes it at a constant $16\ \text{m/s}$. Where does the car catch the bus? (From [2], Example 4.5.)

  1. Car: $x_1 = \frac12at^2$. Bus: $x_2 = v_bt$.

  2. Set $x_1 = x_2$ (ignoring $t = 0$): $t = 2v_b/a = 2(16)/3.0 \approx 11\ \text{s}$.

  3. Position: $x = v_bt = 2v_b^2/a = 2(16)^2/3.0 \approx 1.7\times10^2\ \text{m}$.

Check

At that moment the car moves at $at = 2v_b = 32\ \text{m/s}$, twice the bus's speed, consistent with having equal average velocities over the same time. ✓
