Limits & Derivatives (power rule, chain rule)

What a limit is, how the derivative is defined from it, and the two rules you use most: the power rule and the chain rule.

In short

A limit is "where you are heading". Imagine walking toward a door. Even if you stop one tiny step before it, everyone knows where you were heading: the door. A limit, $\lim_{x\to x_0} f(x)$, is the value $f(x)$ heads toward as $x$ gets closer and closer to $x_0$. What happens exactly at $x_0$ doesn't matter. If the value you're heading to and the value you land on are the same, the function is continuous: you can draw it without lifting your pencil [3].

A derivative is "how fast something changes right now". A car's speedometer shows how fast your position is changing at this very moment. To find it, measure how far the car goes in a short time and divide by that time. Then make the time shorter and shorter. The number you are heading toward (a limit again!) is the derivative [1]; [2]:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)}{\Delta x}.$$

On a graph it is the steepness (slope) of the line that just touches the curve at one point, the tangent [2]. Never just put $\Delta x = 0$ in: you get $0/0$. Simplify first, then let $\Delta x$ shrink [2].

The power rule is a shortcut. For $x^n$, bring the power down in front and lower it by one: $\frac{d}{dx}x^n = n\,x^{n-1}$. So $x^2 \to 2x$ and $x^3 \to 3x^2$ [1]; [2].

The chain rule is for "a function inside a function", like a toy inside a box inside a bigger box. Take the derivative of the outer box (leave the inside alone), then multiply by the derivative of the inside: $\frac{d}{dx}f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$ [1]; [5]. Sums, products and fractions have their own simple rules, listed in the Key formulas below.

Doing it twice gives the second derivative. Position → velocity → acceleration. At the top of a hill, the slope is $0$ (flat), and the second derivative is negative (the curve bends down) [1].

Theory

Limits and continuity

The limit $\lim_{x\to x_0} f(x) = L$ says that $f(x)$ gets as close as we like to $L$ when $x$ is close enough to $x_0$, but not equal to it. The value $f(x_0)$ itself plays no role in the limit.

For many functions you can find the limit just by plugging in $x_0$, for example $\lim_{x\to 3} \frac{x^2+x}{x+1} = \frac{12}{4} = 3$. A function for which this always works at $x_0$ is called continuous there:

$$\lim_{x\to x_0} f(x) = f(x_0).$$

The limit can also be taken from one side only: $x \to x_0^+$ (from the right) or $x \to x_0^-$ (from the left). If the two one-sided limits differ, the function has a jump at $x_0$ and is not continuous there [3].

The derivative as a limit

The derivative of $f$ at $x$ measures how fast the output changes when the input changes. Take a small step $\Delta x$, form the average rate of change (the difference quotient), and let the step shrink to zero [1, §Derivative]; [2]:

$$f'(x) = \frac{df}{dx} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x+\Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x}.$$

Geometrically, the difference quotient is the slope of the secant line through two points of the graph. As the second point slides onto the first, the secant becomes the tangent line, so $f'(x_0)$ is the slope of the tangent at $x_0$. The tangent line there is $y - f(x_0) = f'(x_0)(x - x_0)$ [2].

Physically, the derivative is an instantaneous rate of change. If $y(t)$ is a position, $\Delta y/\Delta t$ is the average velocity over a time interval and $dy/dt$ is the velocity at one instant [2]; [3].

Three notations are common in mechanics: Leibniz $\frac{df}{dx}$, Lagrange $f'(x)$, and Newton's dot $\dot f = \frac{df}{dt}$, which is used only for derivatives with respect to time [1, §Different Notations for Derivatives].

The power rule

For $f(x) = x^n$ with $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$, expand $(x+\Delta x)^n$ with the binomial theorem. Only the first two terms matter:

$$(x+\Delta x)^n = x^n + n x^{n-1}\Delta x + \dots$$

where the dots stand for terms containing $(\Delta x)^2$, $(\Delta x)^3$ and higher powers.

Subtract $x^n$, divide by $\Delta x$, and let $\Delta x \to 0$. Every leftover term still contains a factor of $\Delta x$ and vanishes, so [2]

$$\frac{d}{dx}x^n = n x^{n-1}.$$

The same formula holds for negative powers, $\frac{d}{dx}x^{-n} = -n x^{-n-1}$ (this follows from the chain rule below [5]), and in fact for any real exponent [1, §Derivatives of Common Functions].

Together with the rules for sums, constant multiples, products and quotients, the power rule lets you differentiate any polynomial or rational function [1]; [4]:

$$\begin{gathered} (f+g)' = f' + g', \qquad (cf)' = cf', \\ (fg)' = f'g + fg', \qquad \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}. \end{gathered}$$

The chain rule

A composite function $f(g(x))$ feeds the output of $g$ into $f$. To differentiate it, let $u = g(x)$. A small change $\Delta x$ causes a change $\Delta u$, which in turn causes a change $\Delta y$ in $y = f(u)$. Writing

$$\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta u}\cdot\frac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}$$

and letting $\Delta x \to 0$ (then $\Delta u \to 0$ too, because $g$ is continuous) gives the chain rule [5]; [1]:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\,\frac{du}{dx},$$

or, in Lagrange notation,

$$\frac{d}{dx}f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))\,g'(x).$$

In words: differentiate the outer function, keep the inside unchanged, then multiply by the derivative of the inside. The order matters, since $f(g(x))$ and $g(f(x))$ are different functions in general [5].

Higher derivatives

The derivative of a derivative is the second derivative, $f''(x) = \frac{d^2 f}{dx^2}$, and so on for higher orders [5]. In mechanics this is how acceleration comes from position: $v = \dot x$ and $a = \dot v = \ddot x$.

The first two derivatives also locate extreme values. Where $f'(x) = 0$ the tangent is horizontal; if $f'' < 0$ there, the graph is concave and the point is a maximum, and if $f'' > 0$ it is convex and the point is a minimum [1, §Maxima and Minima, §Concave and Convex Functions].

Note

If $f$ has a derivative at $x_0$, then $f$ is automatically continuous at $x_0$. The reverse is not true: a function can be continuous at a point where its graph has a corner and no tangent [3].

Warning

You cannot find a derivative by setting $\Delta x = 0$ in the difference quotient straight away: that always gives $0/0$. First simplify algebraically so that $\Delta x$ cancels, and only then take the limit [2].

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

Use the definition of the derivative to differentiate $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$. (From [2], Example 1.)

  1. Write the difference quotient:

    $$\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} = \frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\frac{1}{x+\Delta x} - \frac{1}{x}\right).$$

  2. Put the bracket over a common denominator so that $\Delta x$ cancels:

    $$\frac{1}{\Delta x}\cdot\frac{x - (x+\Delta x)}{(x+\Delta x)\,x} = \frac{-1}{(x+\Delta x)\,x}.$$

  3. Now the limit is easy:

    $$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{-1}{(x+\Delta x)\,x} = -\frac{1}{x^2}.$$

Check

The power rule with $n = -1$ gives $\frac{d}{dx}x^{-1} = -x^{-2}$, the same result. The derivative is negative for every $x \ne 0$, which matches the graph of $1/x$: it falls everywhere.

Example

A pumpkin is dropped from a roof. Its height in feet after $t$ seconds is $y(t) = 400 - 16t^2$. Find its average speed during the fall and its velocity when it hits the ground. (From [2], "Physical interpretation of derivatives".)

  1. It lands when $y = 0$: $400 - 16t^2 = 0$, so $t = 5\ \text{s}$.

  2. Average speed = distance / time = $\dfrac{400\ \text{ft}}{5\ \text{s}} = 80\ \text{ft/s}$.

  3. Instantaneous velocity, using the power rule: $\dot y = -32t$. At $t = 5\ \text{s}$, $\dot y = -160\ \text{ft/s}$.

Check

The velocity is negative because the height is decreasing. The impact speed is twice the average speed, as expected when the speed grows linearly from zero.

Example

Differentiate $f(x) = 3x^4 - 5x^2 + 7$ and $q(x) = e^{-x^2}$. (From [1], Worked Example 5(a).)

  1. $f$ is a sum of powers, so use the power, constant and sum rules term by term:

    $$f'(x) = 12x^3 - 10x.$$

  2. $q$ is a composite function: outer $e^u$, inner $u = -x^2$. With $\frac{d}{du}e^u = e^u$ (see the next topic) and $\frac{du}{dx} = -2x$, the chain rule gives

    $$q'(x) = e^{-x^2}\cdot(-2x) = -2x\,e^{-x^2}.$$

Example

A ball's height is $y(t) = 5 + 20t - 5t^2$ (metres, seconds). Find $v(t)$ and $a(t)$, and the time and value of the maximum height. Confirm it is a maximum. (From [1], Worked Example 5(b).)

  1. Differentiate once and twice: $v = \dot y = 20 - 10t$ and $a = \ddot y = -10\ \text{m/s}^2$.

  2. At the top the velocity is zero: $20 - 10t = 0$, so $t = 2\ \text{s}$ and $y(2) = 5 + 40 - 20 = 25\ \text{m}$.

  3. Since $\ddot y = -10 < 0$, the graph is concave, so $t = 2\ \text{s}$ is a maximum.

Check

$y(0) = 5\ \text{m}$ and $y(4) = 5\ \text{m}$: the motion is symmetric about $t = 2\ \text{s}$, as the top of a parabola should be.
