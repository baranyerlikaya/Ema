Cartesian & Polar Coordinates

Two ways to label a point in the plane, with x and y or with a distance r and an angle θ, and how position, velocity and acceleration look in each.

In short

To say where something is, you need a starting point (the origin), some directions (axes) and a way to measure along them [3]. There are two common ways to do it in a flat plane.

Cartesian: "go right, then go up". Like squares on a map: $x$ steps sideways and $y$ steps up. The arrows $\hat\imath$, $\hat\jmath$ (and $\hat k$ for up out of the page) each have length $1$ and point the same way everywhere. That makes them easy to use [1]; [3].

Polar: "turn, then walk". Stand at the origin, turn by an angle $\theta$, and walk a distance $r$. The two ways are linked by a right triangle [3]; [5]:

$$x = r\cos\theta,\quad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}.$$

Angles are measured in radians: an angle $\theta$ cuts an arc $s = R\theta$ from a circle of radius $R$, and a full turn is $2\pi$ [2].

The tricky part. In polar coordinates, the arrows $\hat r$ ("straight out from the centre") and $\hat\theta$ ("around the circle") turn as you move. So when you take derivatives you must also differentiate the arrows. That gives the velocity $\mathbf v = \dot r\,\hat r + r\dot\theta\,\hat\theta$ [4].

Going round in a circle ($r$ constant, steady $\omega = \dot\theta$): the velocity points along the circle with speed $v = r\omega$. The acceleration points to the centre, with size $r\omega^2 = v^2/r$ [2]; [4].

Use Cartesian for straight lines and fixed directions (throwing a ball). Use polar for things turning around a point (a wheel, an orbit) [3]; [4].

Theory

What a coordinate system is

To describe motion with numbers we first fix a coordinate system. That means choosing an origin, a set of axes, a positive direction along each axis, and a set of unit vectors at every point [3, §3.2]. None of these choices is forced by nature: picking them to fit the problem can make a hard problem easy.

Cartesian coordinates

In Cartesian coordinates the axes $x$, $y$, $z$ are mutually perpendicular and meet at the origin. A point is labelled by three numbers $(x, y, z)$, and a vector is written through its components along the axes [1, §Components of Vectors]:

$$\begin{gathered}\mathbf{A} = A_x\,\hat\imath + A_y\,\hat\jmath + A_z\,\hat k, \\ |\mathbf{A}| = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2 + A_z^2}.\end{gathered}$$

The unit vectors $\hat\imath$, $\hat\jmath$, $\hat k$ have length $1$ and point in the directions of increasing $x$, $y$ and $z$ [1, §Unit Vectors]. Their key property is that they are the same at every point in space. Cartesian coordinates are the only system where this is true [3, §3.2.1], and it is what makes differentiating a Cartesian vector so simple: you just differentiate each component.

Polar coordinates

In the plane, a point can instead be labelled by its distance $r \ge 0$ from the origin and the angle $\theta$ between the position vector and the positive $x$-axis, measured counterclockwise. These are the polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ [3, §3.2.2]; [5]. Adding a $z$-axis gives cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$.

From the right triangle formed by $x$, $y$ and $r$ [3, §3.2.2]; [5]:

$$\begin{gathered} x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \\ r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}.\end{gathered}$$

The angle is measured in radians. An angle of $\theta$ radians cuts an arc of length $s = R\theta$ from a circle of radius $R$, so a full turn is $2\pi\ \text{rad} = 360^\circ$ [2, §Circular Motion].

Polar unit vectors

At each point we use two unit vectors: $\hat r$ points radially away from the origin (direction of increasing $r$), and $\hat\theta$ points along the circle in the direction of increasing $\theta$, i.e. counterclockwise [3, §3.2.2]. In terms of the Cartesian unit vectors [4, §6.2]:

$$\hat r = \cos\theta\,\hat\imath + \sin\theta\,\hat\jmath, \qquad \hat\theta = -\sin\theta\,\hat\imath + \cos\theta\,\hat\jmath.$$

Unlike $\hat\imath$ and $\hat\jmath$, these depend on where the point is. For a moving particle $\theta$ changes with time, so $\hat r$ and $\hat\theta$ change too. Differentiating with the chain rule gives [4, §6.2]:

$$\frac{d\hat r}{dt} = \dot\theta\,\hat\theta, \qquad \frac{d\hat\theta}{dt} = -\dot\theta\,\hat r.$$

Velocity and acceleration in polar coordinates

The position vector is simply $\mathbf r = r\,\hat r$. Differentiating with the product rule and the derivatives above gives the velocity [4, §6.6]:

$$\mathbf v = \dot r\,\hat r + r\dot\theta\,\hat\theta.$$

Differentiating once more and collecting terms gives the acceleration [4, §6.6]:

$$\mathbf a = \left(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\right)\hat r + \left(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\right)\hat\theta.$$

For motion on a circle, $r = R$ is constant, so $\dot r = \ddot r = 0$. Then $\mathbf v = R\dot\theta\,\hat\theta$ is tangent to the circle, and for constant $\omega = \dot\theta$ the acceleration is $-R\omega^2\,\hat r$: it points to the centre and has magnitude $R\omega^2 = v^2/R$ [2, §Circular Motion]; [4, §6.3].

Note

Use Cartesian coordinates when the motion is along straight lines or the force has a fixed direction (projectiles, inclined planes). Use polar coordinates when something turns around a fixed point (circular motion, central forces) [3, §3.2]; [4, §6.2].

Warning

$\hat r$ and $\hat\theta$ are not constant vectors. When you differentiate $\mathbf r = r\hat r$, you must differentiate $\hat r$ too. Forgetting this loses the $r\dot\theta\,\hat\theta$ term in the velocity and the $-r\dot\theta^2$ (centripetal) term in the acceleration [4, §6.2, §6.6].

Visualization

A particle moves on a circle with constant angular velocity $\omega$. Its position in polar coordinates is $(r, \theta = \omega t)$; the dashed line is $r\,\hat r$. The dark arrow is the velocity: it points along $\hat\theta$, tangent to the circle, with size $v = \omega r$. The grey arrow is the acceleration: it points along $-\hat r$, toward the centre, with size $a = \omega^2 r$. Change $r$ and $\omega$ and watch both values update.

Key formulas

Worked examples

Example

Find the polar coordinates of the point with Cartesian coordinates $(1, -1)$. (From [5], Example 1.)

  1. $r = \sqrt{1^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt 2$.

  2. The point lies in the fourth quadrant, below the positive $x$-axis, at $45^\circ$. So $\theta = -\pi/4$ (or equivalently $7\pi/4$).

Check

$x = \sqrt2\cos(-\pi/4) = \sqrt2\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2} = 1$ and $y = \sqrt2\sin(-\pi/4) = -1$. ✓

Example

Write the circle of radius $a$ centred at $(a, 0)$ as an equation in polar coordinates. (From [5], Example 2.)

  1. In Cartesian form the circle is $(x-a)^2 + y^2 = a^2$. Substitute $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$:

    $$(r\cos\theta - a)^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta = a^2.$$

  2. Expand and use $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$:

    $$r^2 - 2ar\cos\theta = 0.$$

  3. Divide by $r$: $\;r = 2a\cos\theta$, with $-\pi/2 \le \theta \le \pi/2$ tracing the circle once.

Check

At $\theta = 0$, $r = 2a$: the point $(2a, 0)$, the far side of the circle. At $\theta = \pm\pi/2$, $r = 0$: the origin, which lies on this circle. ✓

Example

A particle moves on a circle of radius $R$ with $\mathbf r(t) = R\cos\theta\,\hat\imath + R\sin\theta\,\hat\jmath$, where $\theta = \omega t + \theta_0$ and $\omega$ is constant. Find its velocity and acceleration. (From [2], §Circular Motion.)

  1. Differentiate each Cartesian component (the unit vectors are constant), using $\dot\theta = \omega$:

    $$\mathbf v = R\omega\left(-\sin\theta\,\hat\imath + \cos\theta\,\hat\jmath\right).$$

  2. The bracket is $\hat\theta$, so $\mathbf v = R\omega\,\hat\theta$: tangent to the circle, with speed $v = R|\omega|$.

  3. Differentiate again:

    $$\begin{aligned}\mathbf a &= -R\omega^2\left(\cos\theta\,\hat\imath + \sin\theta\,\hat\jmath\right) \\ &= -R\omega^2\,\hat r.\end{aligned}$$

    The acceleration points to the centre, with magnitude $a = R\omega^2 = v^2/R$.

Check

The polar formula with $r = R$, $\dot r = 0$, $\ddot\theta = 0$ gives $\mathbf a = -R\dot\theta^2\hat r$, the same result. Also $\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v = 0$: velocity is perpendicular to position, as it must be on a circle.

Example

A particle moves along the spiral $r = b\theta$, with $\theta = ct^2$ ($b, c > 0$). Find its acceleration, and the time at which the radial acceleration is zero. (From [4], Example 6.4.)

  1. Write $r = bct^2$ and compute the four derivatives: $\dot r = 2bct$, $\ddot r = 2bc$, $\dot\theta = 2ct$, $\ddot\theta = 2c$.

  2. Radial component: $a_r = \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 = 2bc - bct^2(2ct)^2 = 2bc - 4bc^3t^4$.

  3. Tangential component: $a_\theta = r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta = 2bc^2t^2 + 8bc^2t^2 = 10bc^2t^2$.

  4. $a_r = 0$ when $4bc^3t^4 = 2bc$, i.e. $t^4 = \dfrac{1}{2c^2}$, so $t_1 = \left(\dfrac{1}{2c^2}\right)^{1/4}$.

Check

At $t_1$ the angle is $\theta_1 = ct_1^2 = c\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2\,c} = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\ \text{rad}$, independent of $b$ and $c$, which matches [4]. Units: $bc$ has units $\text{m}\cdot\text{rad}^{-1}\cdot\text{rad}\cdot\text{s}^{-2} = \text{m/s}^2$. ✓
